## G0027

**Category:** general_autism_knowledge

**Caregiver asked:** What is autism spectrum disorder?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurological and developmental disorder that affects how people interact with others, communicate, learn, and behave. Although autism can be diagnosed at any age, it is described as a “developmental disorder” because symptoms generally appear in the first two years of life. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), a guide created by the American Psychiatric Association that health care providers use to diagnose mental disorders and developmental disorders, people with ASD often have difficulty with social communication and interaction with other people, restricted interests and repetitive behaviors, and symptoms that affect their ability to function in school, work, and other areas of life. Autism is known as a “spectrum” disorder because people with autism have a range of characteristics, needs, strengths, and challenges. People of all ages, races, ethnicities, sexes, and economic backgrounds can be diagnosed with ASD. Although ASD can be a lifelong disorder, treatments, services, and supports can improve a person’s health, well-being, and daily functioning. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that all children receive screening for autism. Caregivers should talk to their child’s health care provider about ASD screening or evaluation.

**Response:**

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by challenges with social interaction, communication, and repetitive behaviors. It is a spectrum disorder, meaning that symptoms can vary widely among individuals, ranging from mild to severe. People with ASD often struggle with understanding and expressing emotions, making friends, and engaging in activities that involve routine or predictability. While there is currently no cure for ASD, early intervention and support can greatly improve outcomes for individuals and families affected by this condition.

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Signs and Symptoms of Autism Spectrum Disorder): People with ASD have behaviors or interests that can seem unusual. These behaviors or interests set ASD apart from conditions defined by problems with social communication and interaction only. Examples of restricted or repetitive behaviors and interests related to ASD can include lines up toys or other objects and gets upset when order is changed; repeats words or phrases over and over (called echolalia); plays with toys the same way every time; is focused on parts of objects (for example, wheels); gets upset by minor changes; has obsessive interests; must follow certain routines; flaps hands, rocks body, or spins self in circles; has unusual reactions to the way things sound, smell, taste, look, or feel.

**Response:**

Restricted or Repetitive Behaviors (RRBs) and Interests (RBIs) are characteristics often associated with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). These can manifest differently across individuals, but here are some common examples:

**Restricted Behaviors:**

* Insisting on strict adherence to routines or rituals
* Repeating movements like hand flapping, rocking, or body swaying
* Having difficulty with changes in daily routines or transitions
* Being overly sensitive to certain sounds, sights, smells, tastes, or textures
* Displaying unusual postures or positions, such as toe-walking or hand-on-the-wall walking
* Engaging in self-soothing activities like hair twirling, nail biting, or skin picking

**Repetitive Interests:**

* Fixating on specific topics, objects, or themes, such as trains, dinosaurs, or video games
* Spending excessive time engaging in preferred activities, like playing video games, watching movies, or reading books
* Memorizing large amounts of information, especially numbers, dates, or lists
* Creating elaborate structures or systems, like building complex Lego creations
* Showing intense interest in collecting items, such as stamps, coins, or sports cards

Keep in mind that these traits don't necessarily indicate an ASD diagnosis. Many people exhibit similar behaviors without having ASD. It's essential to consult a healthcare professional for a comprehensive evaluation before making any conclusions.

## G0031

**Category:** treatment_support

**Caregiver asked:** What are the treatments for autism?

**Reference answer** (source: NICHD What are the treatments for autism?): There is currently no one standard treatment for autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Many people with ASD benefit from treatment, no matter how old they are when they are diagnosed. People of all ages, at all levels of ability, can often improve after well-designed interventions. But there are many ways to help minimize the symptoms and maximize abilities. People who have ASD have the best chance of using all of their abilities and skills if they receive appropriate therapies and interventions. The most effective therapies and interventions are often different for each person. However, most people with ASD respond best to highly structured and specialized programs. In some cases, treatment can greatly reduce symptoms and help people with autism with daily activities. Research shows that early diagnosis and interventions, such as during preschool or before, are more likely to have major positive effects on symptoms and later skills.

**Response:**

There isn't just one single treatment for autism. Instead, various approaches can help individuals with autism make progress and reach their full potential. Effective treatments often involve a combination of therapies tailored to an individual's unique needs and circumstances.

Some commonly used treatments include:

* **Behavioral Management Therapy**: This helps individuals learn new behaviors and replace unwanted ones by breaking down complex tasks into smaller steps.
* **Cognitive Behavior Therapy**: A helpful approach that focuses on identifying and changing negative thought patterns and behaviors.
* **Early Intervention**: Providing support and therapy early in life can significantly impact development and outcomes.
* **Educational and School-Based Therapies**: Tailored educational plans and accommodations can help individuals with autism succeed academically and socially.
* **Joint Attention Therapy**: Encourages shared engagement between two people, promoting social interaction and understanding.
* **Medication Treatment**: May be prescribed to manage related conditions like ADHD, anxiety, or depression.
* **Nutritional Therapy**: Focusing on dietary changes and supplements to address underlying nutritional deficiencies.
* **Occupational Therapy**: Helps develop essential life skills, including daily routines, self-care, and independence.
* **Parent-Mediated Therapy**: Trained parents learn strategies to support their child's growth and development.
* **Physical Therapy**: Enhances physical fitness, coordination, and overall well-being.
* **Social Skills Training**: Teaches essential social interactions, empathy, and relationships.
* **Speech-Language Therapy**: Addresses communication difficulties, improving verbal and non-verbal expression.

It's essential to remember that every individual with autism is unique, and what works best varies greatly from person to person. Consult with a healthcare provider specializing in caring for people with autism to determine the most beneficial course of action for your loved one.
