## G0194

**Category:** diagnosis_boundary

**Caregiver asked:** How is ASD diagnosed in older children and adolescents?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): Caregivers and teachers are often the first to recognize ASD symptoms in older children and adolescents. The school’s special education team may perform an initial evaluation and then recommend that a child receive additional evaluation from their primary health care provider or a health care provider who specializes in ASD. A child’s caregivers may talk with these health care providers about the child’s social difficulties, including problems with subtle communication. These subtle communication differences may include problems understanding tone of voice, facial expressions, or body language. Older children and adolescents may have trouble understanding figures of speech, humor, or sarcasm. They also may have challenges forming friendships with peers. It is also important for the health care provider to learn about the child’s strengths so they can tailor their recommendations for services and supports.

**Response:**

Diagnosing Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) in older children and adolescents involves a comprehensive process that typically includes several steps. Here's an overview:

1. **Comprehensive Clinical Evaluation**: A multidisciplinary team of professionals, including psychologists, psychiatrists, neurologists, speech therapists, occupational therapists, and sometimes social workers, will assess the individual's behavior, communication skills, social interactions, and adaptive behaviors.
2. **Diagnostic Criteria**: Clinicians use standardized criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), which outlines symptoms and characteristics associated with ASD. These include difficulties with social interaction, verbal and non-verbal communication, and repetitive behaviors or interests.
3. **Behavioral Observations**: Trained observers, often parents, teachers, or mental health professionals, record and analyze the individual's behavior over time to gather data on their strengths and challenges.
4. **Developmental History**: Gathering information about the person's early development, including milestones achieved or delayed, helps clinicians better understand their developmental trajectory.
5. **Psychological Assessments**: Standardized tests and assessments, like cognitive and intelligence quotient (IQ) tests, behavioral rating scales, and personality inventories, evaluate various aspects of the individual's functioning.
6. **Medical Evaluations**: Medical professionals examine the individual for potential underlying medical conditions that could contribute to symptoms, such as thyroid disorders, genetic syndromes, or sensory processing issues.
7. **Neuropsychological Testing**: Specialized testing evaluates executive function, memory, attention, and processing speed to determine the extent of any cognitive impairments.
8. **Speech and Language Assessment**: Speech-language pathologists assess the individual's communication abilities, including verbal and non-verbal skills.
9. **Occupational Therapy Evaluation**: Occupational therapy specialists observe and assess the individual's daily living activities, fine motor skills, and ability to adapt to changes in routine.
10. **Collaborative Diagnosis**: After gathering all this information, the multidisciplinary team meets to discuss findings, consider multiple perspectives, and reach a collaborative diagnosis.

**Important Note:** Diagnosing ASD in older children and adolescents can be more challenging than diagnosing younger children due to the presence of learned coping mechanisms and masking strategies. However, early intervention and support remain crucial for optimal outcomes.

If you're seeking a diagnosis for yourself or a loved one, consult with a healthcare provider or a licensed therapist who specializes in ASD evaluations. They'll guide you through the process and ensure you receive the necessary support and resources.

**Caregiver asked:** Do vaccines cause or worsen mitochondrial diseases?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Frequently Asked Questions about Autism Spectrum Disorder): As of now, there are no scientific studies that say vaccines cause or worsen mitochondrial diseases. We do know certain illnesses that can be prevented by vaccines, such as the flu, can trigger the regression that is related to a mitochondrial disease. However, we know that for most children, vaccines are a safe and important way to prevent life-threatening diseases.
