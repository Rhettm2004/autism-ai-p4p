## G0259

**Category:** safety_vaccines

**Caregiver asked:** Do vaccines cause or worsen mitochondrial diseases?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Frequently Asked Questions about Autism Spectrum Disorder): As of now, there are no scientific studies that say vaccines cause or worsen mitochondrial diseases. We do know certain illnesses that can be prevented by vaccines, such as the flu, can trigger the regression that is related to a mitochondrial disease. More research is needed to determine if there are rare cases where underlying mitochondrial disorders are triggered by anything related to vaccines. However, we know that for most children, vaccines are a safe and important way to prevent life-threatening diseases.

**Response:**

Vaccines do not cause or worsen mitochondrial disorders. According to the World Health Organization's (WHO) Autism Questions and Answers:

"Mitochondrial myopathies are a group of rare inherited disorders caused by problems with the mitochondria, which are tiny structures within cells that produce energy."

The WHO states that "extensive research has shown that there is no evidence that vaccines cause mitochondrial disorders." They further clarify that while some children with mitochondrial disorders may experience adverse reactions to vaccinations, this is due to their underlying disorder, not the vaccination itself.

It's essential to note that individuals with pre-existing mitochondrial disorders should consult their healthcare provider before receiving vaccinations to discuss potential risks and benefits. However, the WHO emphasizes that "the vast majority of children with mitochondrial disorders can safely receive standard vaccinations."

If you're concerned about your child's specific situation, please consult with your pediatrician or healthcare provider for personalized advice.

## G0260

**Category:** diagnosis_boundary

**Caregiver asked:** How is ASD diagnosed in adults?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): Diagnosing ASD in adults is often more difficult than diagnosing ASD in children. In adults, some ASD symptoms can overlap with symptoms of mental disorders such as an anxiety disorder or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Adults who have questions about whether they may be on the autism spectrum should talk with a health care provider and ask for a referral for an ASD evaluation. Although evaluation for ASD in adults is still being refined, adults can be referred to a neuropsychologist, psychologist, or psychiatrist who has experience with ASD. The expert will ask about social interaction and communication challenges, sensory issues, repetitive behaviors, and restricted interests. The evaluation may also include a conversation with caregivers and family members to learn about the person’s early developmental history, which can help ensure an accurate diagnosis. Obtaining a correct diagnosis of ASD as an adult can help people understand past challenges, identify personal strengths, and find the right kind of help. Studies are underway to determine the types of services and supports that are most helpful for autistic transition-age youth and adults.

**Response:**

Diagnosing ASD (Autism Spectrum Disorder) in adults can be challenging due to overlapping symptoms with other mental disorders such as anxiety or ADHD. To diagnose ASD in adults, healthcare providers typically evaluate social interaction and communication challenges, sensory issues, repetitive behaviors, and restricted interests. An expert in ASD, such as a neuropsychologist or psychologist with experience in ASD, conducts the evaluation.
