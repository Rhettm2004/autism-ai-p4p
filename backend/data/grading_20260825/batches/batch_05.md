**Reference answer** (source: WHO Autism Questions and Answers): No. Extensive research has shown that vaccines do not cause autism. Many large, high-quality studies conducted in different countries and involving large populations have all reached the same conclusion. Research using a variety of different methods and conducted over many years has demonstrated that the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine does not cause autism. The study that was interpreted as indicating any such link was later proven to be wrong and fraudulent. The journal that published it withdrew the study, and the doctor who authored it lost his medical license. Evidence also shows that other childhood vaccines do not increase the risk of autism. Extensive research into the preservative thiomersal and the additive aluminium that are contained in some vaccines strongly concluded that these constituents in childhood vaccines do not increase the risk of autism.

**Response:**

There's been a lot of concern and misinformation about vaccines and autism. The idea that there might be a link between the two originated from a 1998 study published in the Lancet, but it was later found to be flawed and retracted.

Since then, numerous studies have investigated this potential connection, and none have found any evidence to support a causal relationship between vaccines and autism. In fact, many reputable health organizations worldwide, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the World Health Organization (WHO), and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), all agree: there is no scientific basis for a link between vaccines and autism.

The overwhelming consensus among medical experts is that vaccines are safe and effective in preventing serious diseases like measles, mumps, rubella, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, hepatitis B, and more. These diseases can cause severe illness or even death if left untreated.

## G0113

**Category:** treatment_support

**Caregiver asked:** What are behavioral approaches for autism spectrum disorder?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Treatment and Intervention for Autism Spectrum Disorder): Behavioral approaches focus on changing behaviors by understanding what happens before and after the behavior. Behavioral approaches have the most evidence for treating symptoms of ASD. They have become widely accepted among educators and healthcare professionals and are used in many schools and treatment clinics. A notable behavioral treatment for people with ASD is called applied behavior analysis (ABA). ABA encourages desired behaviors and discourages undesired behaviors to improve a variety of skills.

**Response:**

Behavioral approaches for Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) focus on understanding and supporting the individual's unique needs, preferences, and behaviors. These approaches aim to help individuals with ASD develop skills, manage challenging behaviors, and improve their quality of life.

Some common behavioral approaches include:

1. **Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA)**: ABA is a well-established approach that focuses on breaking down complex behaviors into smaller, manageable parts. It uses positive reinforcement techniques, such as rewards and praise, to encourage desired behaviors.
2. **Positive Behavioral Supports (PBS)**: PBS emphasizes creating a supportive environment that promotes positive behaviors and reduces problem behaviors. This approach involves identifying triggers for challenging behaviors and developing strategies to prevent or address them.
3. **Functional Communication Training**: This approach helps individuals with ASD learn alternative communication methods, such as augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), to express their needs and wants more effectively.
4. **Pivotal Response Treatment (PRT)**: PRT focuses on teaching new skills by using natural consequences, such as ignoring undesired behavior and reinforcing desired behavior.
5. **Early Start Denver Model (ESDM)**: ESDM combines play-based interventions with evidence-based practices to support early childhood development in children with ASD.
6. **Floortime**: Floortime is an approach developed by Stanley Greenspan, which encourages caregivers to engage with children at their level, promoting emotional connection, social interaction, and learning through play.
7. **Relationship Development Intervention (RDI)**: RDI aims to strengthen relationships between family members and individuals with ASD, focusing on building trust, empathy, and effective communication.

These behavioral approaches often involve working closely with trained professionals, such as psychologists, therapists, and educators, who can tailor programs to meet the individual's specific needs and goals.

Keep in mind that every person with ASD is unique, and what works best may vary from one individual to another. It's essential to consult with a qualified professional to determine the most suitable approach for your child or loved one.

If you're concerned about someone's developmental progress or would like to explore these approaches further, I recommend speaking with your GP or pediatrician, who can refer you to a specialist for guidance and support.
