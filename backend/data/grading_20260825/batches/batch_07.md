## G0157

**Category:** screening_item_clarification

**Caregiver asked:** What are examples of restricted or repetitive behaviors and interests related to ASD?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Signs and Symptoms of Autism Spectrum Disorder): People with ASD have behaviors or interests that can seem unusual. These behaviors or interests set ASD apart from conditions defined by problems with social communication and interaction only. Examples of restricted or repetitive behaviors and interests related to ASD can include lines up toys or other objects and gets upset when order is changed; repeats words or phrases over and over (called echolalia); plays with toys the same way every time; is focused on parts of objects (for example, wheels); gets upset by minor changes; has obsessive interests; must follow certain routines; flaps hands, rocks body, or spins self in circles; has unusual reactions to the way things sound, smell, taste, look, or feel.

**Response:**

Restricted or repetitive behaviors and interests are common in people with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). These can include:

* Repetitive movements, such as hand flapping, rocking back and forth, or body swaying
* Insistence on strict adherence to routines or rituals, like following a specific daily schedule or having things done in a certain order
* Strong attachment to specific objects, toys, or activities that may seem unusual or intense to others
* Preoccupation with details of an object or activity, often at the expense of other aspects of life
* Difficulty with changes in routine or unexpected events

These behaviors might look different from typical child development. For example, some children might be very particular about their food, only eating foods that fit within a narrow range, while another child might have strong preferences for playing with trains or watching videos.

It's essential to remember that these behaviors alone do not confirm autism. If you're concerned about your child's behavior, it's always best to speak with their healthcare provider about getting screened for ASD.

If you've noticed any of these signs, I recommend speaking with your child's healthcare provider about scheduling an evaluation. They will assess your child's overall health and development and provide guidance on what to expect next.

## G0158

**Category:** safety_vaccines

**Caregiver asked:** Do vaccines cause autism spectrum disorder (ASD)?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Frequently Asked Questions about Autism Spectrum Disorder): Many studies have looked at whether there is a relationship between vaccines and ASD. To date, the studies continue to show that vaccines are not associated with ASD. CDC knows some parents and others still have concerns. To address these concerns, CDC is part of the Inter-Agency Autism Coordinating Committee (IACC), which is working with the National Vaccine Advisory Committee (NVAC) on this issue. The job of the NVAC is to advise and make recommendations regarding the National Vaccine Program. Communication between the IACC and the NVAC will allow each group to share skills and knowledge, improve coordination, and promote better use of research resources on vaccine topics.

**Response:**

There is a lot of misinformation out there about vaccines and autism. The scientific consensus is clear: vaccines do not cause autism.

The idea that vaccines might be linked to autism originated from a now-discredited study published in 1998 by Andrew Wakefield, which suggested a link between the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine and autism. However, this study was later found to be flawed and retracted due to serious methodological errors and conflicts of interest.

Since then, numerous studies have been conducted to investigate any potential connection between vaccines and autism. These studies have consistently shown no association between vaccination and an increased risk of developing autism.

In fact, many reputable health organizations worldwide, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the World Health Organization (WHO), and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), all agree that vaccines are safe and effective in preventing serious diseases like measles, mumps, polio, and more.

It's essential to note that some children may experience side effects after receiving vaccinations, such as pain or redness at the injection site, but these reactions are usually mild and temporary.

If you're concerned about your child's development or suspect they might be showing signs of autism, it's crucial to consult with their pediatrician or a qualified healthcare professional. They can assess your child's overall health and provide guidance on what to expect during regular check-ups and developmental milestones.

Remember, if you have concerns about your child's development, don't hesitate to reach out to your child's doctor.

## G0163

**Category:** treatment_support

**Caregiver asked:** What are behavioral approaches for autism spectrum disorder?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Treatment and Intervention for Autism Spectrum Disorder): Behavioral approaches focus on changing behaviors by understanding what happens before and after the behavior. Behavioral approaches have the most evidence for treating symptoms of ASD. They have become widely accepted among educators and healthcare professionals and are used in many schools and treatment clinics. A notable behavioral treatment for people with ASD is called applied behavior analysis (ABA). ABA encourages desired behaviors and discourages undesired behaviors to improve a variety of skills.

**Response:**

Behavioral approaches for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) are evidence-based interventions that aim to improve social communication and behavior in individuals with ASD. These approaches focus on teaching skills and behaviors through structured instruction, reinforcement, and practice. Some common behavioral approaches for ASD include:

1. Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA): ABA is a systematic approach that uses positive reinforcement to teach new behaviors and reduce problematic behaviors. It involves breaking down complex behaviors into smaller, more manageable steps and using rewards or tokens to motivate the individual to perform each step correctly.
2. Social Skills Training (SST): SST is an intervention that focuses specifically on improving social communication and interaction skills. It typically involves role-playing exercises, group activities, and feedback from peers and instructors.
3. Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT): CBT is a type of talk therapy that helps individuals identify and change negative thought patterns and behaviors. In the context of ASD, CBT may be used to address anxiety, depression, and other mental health concerns related to ASD.
4. Play-Based Therapy: Play-based therapy involves using play as a tool for learning and skill development. This approach can be particularly effective for children with ASD who have difficulty with traditional forms of instruction.
5. Communication Support: Individuals with ASD often struggle with verbal communication, so communication support may involve the use of assistive technology, such as speech-generating devices, or alternative communication methods, such as sign language or picture exchange communication systems.

It's important to note that while these approaches can be helpful for individuals with ASD, they should not replace medical treatment or diagnosis. If you suspect your child has ASD, it's important to seek a professional evaluation from a qualified clinician, such as a developmental pediatrician or psychologist.

Genes play a big role in this. Environmental factors might also play a part. Some studies suggest that exposure to certain things during pregnancy or early life could increase the risk of developing ASD. These include:

- Advanced parental age
- Maternal diabetes during pregnancy
- Prenatal exposure to air pollutants or heavy metals
- Premature birth
- Severe birth complications
- Low birth weight

It's essential to remember that every child with autism is different, and we still need to learn more about the causes of this condition. If you're concerned about your child's development, I recommend speaking with a healthcare professional for personalized guidance and support.

- Social interaction
- Gross motor skills (like crawling, walking)

## G0181

**Category:** screening_limitations

**Caregiver asked:** Should I correct for prematurity?

**Reference answer** (source: M-CHAT Parent and Professional FAQ): We do not recommend adjusting for prematurity. In part this is because many standardized tests only correct for the first year, and in part because adjusting for prematurity may decrease sensitivity. We are aware that the screen positive rate is elevated in samples of premature children, but evidence shows this is due to an elevated prevalence of autism in these cases. See Hamer et al., 2024, Developmental Medicine and Child Neurology.

**Response:**

When using the M-CHAT-R/F, it's essential to consider whether your child is premature or has a history of developmental delays. If that's the case, you should indeed correct for prematurity.

The M-CHAT-R/F allows you to adjust the scoring based on the child's gestational age at birth and their current chronological age. This adjustment helps ensure that the results are accurate and not skewed by any potential developmental differences due to prematurity.

To correct for prematurity:

1.  Identify your child's gestational age (weeks) at birth.
2. Calculate how many weeks they were premature (if applicable).
3. Use this information in the M-CHAT-R/ F calculator to get an adjusted score.

Keep in mind that correcting for prematurity doesn't change the fact that only a qualified professional can provide a diagnosis. A high-risk result still indicates that further evaluation might be helpful, but it does not confirm autism.

If you have concerns about your child's development, I recommend discussing these findings with a pediatrician or a specialist who can help determine the next steps.

## G0182

**Category:** screening_follow_up

**Caregiver asked:** If my child screened negative (passed the screener), does that mean he cannot have a developmental concern?

**Reference answer** (source: M-CHAT Parent and Professional FAQ): No. The M-CHAT-R/F does not screen for all developmental delays or concerns. Also, a screening tool cannot be perfect. If you have concerns, you should talk to your child’s healthcare professional. You also can contact your local early intervention agency to request an evaluation of eligibility for early intervention services for your child. If you are in the US you can find your state’s intervention office here: https://www.autismspeaks.org/resource-guide and select “State Early Intervention Providers”.

**Response:**

A negative result on a screening tool, such as the M-CHAT-R/F or Q-CHAT-10, does not mean your child cannot have any developmental concerns. It's essential to understand that these tools are designed to identify children who may benefit from further evaluation, but they're not perfect and can miss some cases.

Think of it like a traffic light: green means "go" (no concern), yellow means "caution" (higher risk), and red means "stop" (definitely investigate). A negative result is more like a green light – it doesn't necessarily indicate everything is fine; rather, it suggests there might be no immediate cause for concern based on this specific test.

However, every child develops at their own pace, and some might need extra support even if they pass a screener. If you notice any unusual delays in development, changes in behavior, or other signs of potential difficulties, don't hesitate to discuss them with your child's doctor or healthcare provider. They will help determine whether additional evaluations or assessments are needed.

It's also worth noting that passing a screener doesn't rule out the possibility of autism or other conditions entirely. Only a qualified professional can provide an accurate diagnosis after conducting a comprehensive assessment.
