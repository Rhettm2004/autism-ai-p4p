## G0184

**Category:** general_autism_knowledge

**Caregiver asked:** What causes autism?

**Reference answer** (source: WHO Autism Questions and Answers): Scientific evidence suggests that various factors, both genetic and environmental, contribute to the onset of autism by influencing early brain development. Studies show an increased likelihood of autism for families with an autistic member and particularly for identical twins. Scientific studies show that exposure to certain environmental factors appear to occur more frequently in children with autism, or their parents. These include advanced parental age, maternal diabetes during pregnancy, prenatal exposure to air pollutants or certain heavy metals, prematurity, severe birth complications and low birth weight. In addition, research studies have looked into a possible association between use of various medicines during pregnancy and increased risk for autism. For example, prenatal exposure to valproate and carbamazepine, which are used for seizures, appear to occur more frequently in children with autism. More research is needed to better understand the role of each of the factors that appear to be associated with a higher risk for autism, and how they interact with genetic variations. Autism is not caused by bad parenting or vaccines and vaccine ingredients, and is not linked to diet. It is also not an infection and cannot be spread to other people.

**Response:**

Scientists are still studying the causes of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and it is likely that there is a combination of genetic and environmental factors that contribute to its onset. Genetic mutations, chromosomal abnormalities, exposure to certain environmental factors during pregnancy or early childhood, and advanced parental age are some of the possible contributing factors. More research is needed to better understand the underlying causes of ASD.

## G0192

**Category:** treatment_support

**Caregiver asked:** What are behavioral approaches for autism spectrum disorder?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Treatment and Intervention for Autism Spectrum Disorder): Behavioral approaches focus on changing behaviors by understanding what happens before and after the behavior. Behavioral approaches have the most evidence for treating symptoms of ASD. They have become widely accepted among educators and healthcare professionals and are used in many schools and treatment clinics. A notable behavioral treatment for people with ASD is called applied behavior analysis (ABA). ABA encourages desired behaviors and discourages undesired behaviors to improve a variety of skills.

**Response:**

There are several behavioral approaches for autism spectrum disorder (ASD), which aim to help individuals with ASD improve their social communication and interaction skills. Some of the most commonly used approaches include:

1. Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA): This is a systematic approach that uses positive reinforcement, shaping, and other techniques to teach new behaviors and reduce problematic ones. It has been shown to be effective in improving social communication and interaction skills in individuals with ASD.
2. Social Skills Training (SST): This approach focuses on teaching individuals with ASD how to interact socially in various settings, such as school or work. SST typically involves role-playing exercises, group activities, and individual coaching.
3. Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT): This approach helps individuals with ASD identify and change negative thought patterns and behaviors that may interfere with social communication and interaction. CBT can also help individuals develop coping strategies for dealing with anxiety and stress.
4. Play-Based Therapy: This approach uses play as a tool for promoting social communication and interaction skills, particularly in children with ASD. Play-based therapy often involves structured play activities that encourage social interaction and cooperation.
5. Communication Support: Individuals with ASD may have difficulty communicating verbally, so alternative forms of communication support, such as picture exchange communication systems (PECS) or speech-generating devices, may be helpful.

It's important to note that these approaches should not be used in isolation but rather as part of a comprehensive treatment plan tailored to an individual's specific needs and strengths. Additionally, it's essential to seek professional guidance from a qualified therapist or clinician when implementing any behavioral approach for autism spectrum disorder.

**Caregiver asked:** How is ASD diagnosed in adults?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): Diagnosing ASD in adults is often more difficult than diagnosing ASD in children. In adults, some ASD symptoms can overlap with symptoms of mental disorders such as an anxiety disorder or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Adults who have questions about whether they may be on the autism spectrum should talk with a health care provider and ask for a referral for an ASD evaluation. Although evaluation for ASD in adults is still being refined, adults can be referred to a neuropsychologist, psychologist, or psychiatrist who has experience with ASD. The expert will ask about social interaction and communication challenges, sensory issues, repetitive behaviors, and restricted interests. The evaluation may also include a conversation with caregivers and family members to learn about the person’s early developmental history, which can help ensure an accurate diagnosis. Obtaining a correct diagnosis of ASD as an adult can help people understand past challenges, identify personal strengths, and find the right kind of help. Studies are underway to determine the types of services and supports that are most helpful for autistic transition-age youth and adults.

**Response:**

Diagnosing Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) in adults is a bit different from diagnosing it in children. In the past, ASD was often considered a childhood disorder, and many people were not diagnosed until they reached adulthood.

In recent years, there has been an increased recognition of autism across all ages, including adults. Diagnosing ASD in adults involves a comprehensive evaluation by a qualified professional, such as a psychologist or psychiatrist with expertise in adult neurodevelopmental disorders.

The diagnostic process for adults typically includes:

1.  **Comprehensive clinical interview**: A thorough discussion about your symptoms, medical history, and personal experiences.
2.  **Psychological assessments**: Standardized tests and questionnaires to evaluate cognitive function, emotional regulation, social interaction, and communication skills.
3.  **Medical examination**: A physical exam to rule out any underlying medical conditions that might be contributing to symptoms.
4.  **Review of developmental history**: An exploration of your early development, including family history, birth order, and significant life events.
5.  **Behavioral observations**: Observations of your behavior, interactions, and coping mechanisms.

Some common tools used in adult ASD diagnosis include:

* The Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-Adult Version (ADOS-A)
* The Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ)
* The Ritvo-Folstein Scale
* The Adult Asperger Assessment (AAA)

A diagnosis of ASD in adults can have a significant impact on daily life, relationships, and mental health. If you're concerned about yourself or someone else's potential ASD diagnosis, I recommend speaking with a healthcare provider or a specialist who can guide you through this process.

Keep in mind that only a qualified professional can diagnose ASD. While online screening tools are available, these should not be relied upon for a definitive diagnosis.
