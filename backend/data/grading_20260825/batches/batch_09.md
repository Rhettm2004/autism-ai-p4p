## G0209

**Category:** diagnosis_boundary

**Caregiver asked:** How is ASD diagnosed in adults?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): Diagnosing ASD in adults is often more difficult than diagnosing ASD in children. In adults, some ASD symptoms can overlap with symptoms of mental disorders such as an anxiety disorder or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Adults who have questions about whether they may be on the autism spectrum should talk with a health care provider and ask for a referral for an ASD evaluation. Although evaluation for ASD in adults is still being refined, adults can be referred to a neuropsychologist, psychologist, or psychiatrist who has experience with ASD. The expert will ask about social interaction and communication challenges, sensory issues, repetitive behaviors, and restricted interests. The evaluation may also include a conversation with caregivers and family members to learn about the person’s early developmental history, which can help ensure an accurate diagnosis. Obtaining a correct diagnosis of ASD as an adult can help people understand past challenges, identify personal strengths, and find the right kind of help. Studies are underway to determine the types of services and supports that are most helpful for autistic transition-age youth and adults.

**Response:**

Diagnosing Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) in adults is a bit different from diagnosing it in children. In the past, ASD was often associated with childhood, but research has shown that many people receive an autism diagnosis as adults.

In adults, a comprehensive diagnostic process typically involves:

1.  **Self-referral or referral by a healthcare provider**: Many adults seek out evaluation and support on their own, while others may be referred by family members, friends, employers, or mental health professionals.
2.  **Comprehensive clinical assessment**: A trained professional, such as a psychologist, psychiatrist, or neurologist, conducts a thorough evaluation to gather information about the adult's history, behavior, and experiences. This might include:
    *   Medical and psychological questionnaires
    *   Interviews to discuss symptoms, behaviors, and life experiences
    *   Review of medical and educational records
3.  **Diagnostic criteria**: The clinician uses standardized tools and guidelines, like those outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), to assess whether the individual meets the diagnostic criteria for ASD.
4.  **Multidisciplinary team involvement**: Depending on the complexity of the case, a multidisciplinary team might involve specialists from various fields, including psychology, psychiatry, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology, and social work.
5.  **Diagnostic report and recommendations**: After completing the evaluation, the clinician provides a detailed report outlining the findings, diagnosis, and recommended next steps. If diagnosed with ASD, this might include suggestions for accommodations, therapies, and support services.

Some common reasons why adults seek an ASD diagnosis include:

*   Difficulty with relationships or social interactions
*   Challenges with employment or education due to difficulties with communication, executive functioning, or sensory processing
*   Unexplained anxiety, depression, or other mental health concerns
*   Sensory sensitivities or differences

If you're considering seeking an ASD diagnosis as an adult, consult with your primary care physician or a trusted healthcare provider for guidance on finding resources and specialists in your area.

**Caregiver asked:** Are all children routinely tested for mitochondrial diseases? What about children with autism?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Frequently Asked Questions about Autism Spectrum Disorder): Children are not routinely tested for mitochondrial diseases. This includes children with autism and other developmental delays. Testing is not easy and may involve taking multiple samples of blood, and often samples of muscle. Doctors decide whether testing for mitochondrial diseases should be done based on a child's signs and symptoms.

**Response:**

Children are not routinely tested for mitochondrial diseases, but there may be specific situations where a child would undergo testing. Mitochondrial disease is a group of disorders caused by problems with the mitochondria, which are tiny structures within cells that produce energy.

Some children might get tested if they have symptoms or signs that could suggest a mitochondrial disorder, such as:

- Muscle weakness
- Fatigue
- Poor growth
- Developmental delays

However, this is not routine screening like what happens in autism screenings.

Regarding children with autism: There isn't a standard test to screen all children for autism at birth or during early childhood. However, some high-risk groups receive extra attention and monitoring due to their family history or other factors. These include:

1. Siblings of children already diagnosed with autism.
2. Children born prematurely (before 37 weeks).
3. Those who experience developmental delays or regression after an initial normal development assessment.
4. Infants exposed to certain environmental toxins or infections during pregnancy.

In these cases, healthcare providers often use specialized tools like the M-CHAT-R/F or Q-CHAT-10 to assess the child's social communication skills and behaviors. If concerns arise from these assessments, further evaluation by a qualified professional is recommended.

Keep in mind that only a qualified clinician can diagnose autism spectrum disorder based on comprehensive evaluations and medical histories.

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Frequently Asked Questions about Autism Spectrum Disorder): Yes, adults can be diagnosed with ASD. Diagnosis includes looking at the person's medical history, watching the person's behavior, and giving the person some psychological tests. But it can be more challenging to diagnose an adult because it is not always possible to know about the person's development during the first few years of life, and a long history of other diagnoses may complicate an ASD diagnosis. Because the focus of ASD research has been on children, we still have much to learn about the prevalence and causes of ASD across the lifespan. Behavioral interventions can be effective for adults coping with a new diagnosis of autism.

**Response:**

Yes, adults can be diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). While symptoms often appear in early childhood, many people are not formally diagnosed until later in life. This is because the diagnostic criteria for ASD were developed primarily based on children's behaviors and development.

In fact, research suggests that some individuals may not receive a diagnosis of ASD until they reach adulthood or even middle age. This delayed diagnosis can occur due to various reasons such as:

* Masking: Some individuals might have learned to hide their autistic traits through social learning and adaptation.

* Lack of awareness: Until recently, there was less understanding about adult-onset ASD, leading to underdiagnosis.

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) allows for diagnoses in all ages, including adults. If you're concerned about your own or someone else's potential ASD diagnosis, it's essential to speak with a qualified professional who can assess and evaluate individual circumstances.
