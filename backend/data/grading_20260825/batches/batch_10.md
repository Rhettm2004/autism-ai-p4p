## G0236

**Category:** treatment_support

**Caregiver asked:** What treatments and services are available for ASD?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): Interventions, services, and supports for ASD are most effective when they begin as soon as possible after diagnosis. Receiving appropriate care and services can help address a person’s specific needs and challenges while also helping them learn new skills and build on their strengths. People with ASD have a wide range of issues and needs, which means there is no single best approach. Working closely with health care and service providers is an important part of finding the right combination of interventions, services, and supports.

**Response:**

There are a variety of treatments and services available for Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). These can include:

1. Behavioral therapy: This type of therapy focuses on helping individuals with ASD learn new skills, behaviors, and social interactions through structured activities and exercises. Examples include Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) and Social Skills Training (SST).
2. Communication therapy: This type of treatment helps individuals with ASD improve their communication skills, including verbal and nonverbal communication, language development, and speech therapy.
3. Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT): This type of therapy is used to help individuals with ASD manage anxiety, depression, and other mental health issues that may be associated with the condition.
4. Occupational therapy: This type of service helps individuals with ASD develop daily living skills such as dressing, grooming, and feeding themselves.
5. Educational support: Individuals with ASD may benefit from specialized educational programs or accommodations in school to help them succeed academically.
6. Support groups: Joining a support group can provide emotional support and practical advice for parents and caregivers of individuals with ASD.
7. Medical interventions: In some cases, medical interventions such as medication or dietary changes may be recommended to address symptoms associated with ASD. However, these should only be prescribed by a qualified healthcare professional.

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Frequently Asked Questions about Autism Spectrum Disorder): A child with a mitochondrial disease may also have an autism spectrum disorder, may have some of the symptoms or signs of autism, or may not have any signs or symptoms related to autism. In general, more research about mitochondrial disease and autism is needed.

**Response:**

There's a connection between mitochondrial disease and autism, but it's complex.

Mitochondrial diseases are conditions where the mitochondria (the energy-producing structures within cells) don't work correctly. This can lead to problems with energy production in various parts of the body.

Research suggests that some people with certain types of mitochondrial disease may be at higher risk for developing autism spectrum disorder (ASD). However, this is not a direct cause-and-effect relationship.

Some studies have found that children with mitochondrial disease might also have an increased likelihood of having ASD or other developmental delays. But many children with mitochondrial disease do not develop ASD.

The exact reasons behind this link aren't fully understood yet. It's thought that issues related to energy production in the brain could contribute to difficulties with development and social interaction, which are common characteristics of ASD.

If you're concerned about your child's health or their potential risk factors, I recommend speaking with a qualified healthcare professional who can assess them individually. They'll consider all relevant information, including any family history of mitochondrial disease, when evaluating your child's overall health.

## G0256

**Category:** general_health_knowledge

**Caregiver asked:** Is there a relationship between autism and encephalopathy?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Frequently Asked Questions about Autism Spectrum Disorder): Most children with an autism spectrum disorder do not have and have not had an encephalopathy. Some children with an autism spectrum disorder have had regression and some have had a regressive encephalopathy.

**Response:**

No, there is no relationship between autism and encephalopathy. Encephalopathy is a condition that affects the brain and can cause symptoms such as seizures, confusion, and difficulty with memory or thinking. Autism is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by difficulties in social interaction, communication, and repetitive behaviors. While some people with autism may have epilepsy or other neurological conditions, autism itself is not an encephalopathic disorder.

## G0257

**Category:** screening_item_clarification

**Caregiver asked:** What are examples of social communication and social interaction characteristics related to ASD?

**Reference answer** (source: CDC Signs and Symptoms of Autism Spectrum Disorder): Social communication and interaction skills can be challenging for people with ASD. Examples of social communication and social interaction characteristics related to ASD can include avoids or does not keep eye contact; does not respond to name by 9 months of age; does not show facial expressions such as happy, sad, angry, and surprised by 9 months of age; does not play simple interactive games like pat-a-cake by 12 months of age; uses few or no gestures by 12 months of age; does not share interests with others by 15 months of age; does not point to show you something interesting by 18 months of age; does not notice when others are hurt or upset by 24 months (2 years) of age; does not notice other children and join them in play by 36 months (3 years) of age; does not pretend to be something else, like a teacher or superhero, during play by 48 months (4 years) of age; does not sing, dance, or act for you by 60 months (5 years) of age.

**Response:**

Examples of social communication and social interaction characteristics related to ASD include:

1. Difficulty understanding nonverbal cues such as facial expressions, body language, and tone of voice.
2. Struggling with making and maintaining eye contact.
3. Tendency to take things literally or interpret statements in a very direct way.
4. Difficulty understanding sarcasm, humor, and other forms of figurative language.
5. Struggling with initiating and sustaining conversations.
6. Difficulty understanding social rules and norms.
7. Tendency to repeat phrases or questions.
8. Struggling with understanding and responding appropriately to greetings and goodbyes.
9. Difficulty understanding and interpreting social situations.
10. Struggling with expressing emotions and understanding the emotions of others.

## G0258

**Category:** diagnosis_boundary

**Caregiver asked:** What happens during an additional diagnostic evaluation?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): It is important to accurately detect and diagnose children with ASD as early as possible, as this will shed light on their unique strengths and challenges. Early detection can also help caregivers determine which services, educational programs, and behavioral therapies are most likely to be helpful for their child. A team of health care providers who have experience diagnosing ASD will conduct the diagnostic evaluation. This team may include child neurologists, developmental behavioral pediatricians, speech-language pathologists, child psychologists and psychiatrists, educational specialists, and occupational therapists. The diagnostic evaluation is likely to include medical and neurological examinations, assessment of the child’s cognitive abilities, assessment of the child’s speech and language abilities, observation of the child’s behavior, an in-depth conversation with the child’s caregivers about the child’s behavior and development, assessment of age-appropriate skills needed to complete daily activities independently, such as eating, dressing, and toileting, and questions about the child’s family history. Because ASD is a complex disorder that sometimes occurs with other conditions or learning disorders, the comprehensive evaluation may include blood tests and a hearing test. The outcome of this evaluation may result in a formal diagnosis and recommendations for treatment.

**Response:**

During an additional diagnostic evaluation, a qualified clinician will conduct a comprehensive assessment to determine if your child has autism or another developmental condition. This may include observing your child's behavior and interactions with others, conducting interviews with you and other caregivers, reviewing medical history, and possibly using standardized tests like the Q-CHAT-10 and M-CHAT-R/F.

The goal of this evaluation is to gather as much information as possible about your child's strengths and challenges in various areas, such as communication, social interaction, and restricted interests or behaviors. Based on this information, the clinician will make a diagnosis, which may be autism spectrum disorder (ASD), another developmental condition, or no diagnosis at all.

It's important to remember that a screening result does not confirm or rule out autism. A high-risk score simply suggests that further investigation is needed. If your child receives a high-risk screening result, it's crucial to follow up with a professional for a proper diagnosis and treatment plan.

## G0260

**Category:** diagnosis_boundary

**Caregiver asked:** How is ASD diagnosed?

**Reference answer** (source: NIMH Autism Spectrum Disorder): Health care providers diagnose ASD by evaluating a person’s behavior and development. ASD can usually be reliably diagnosed by the age of 2. It is important to seek an evaluation as soon as possible. The earlier ASD is diagnosed, the sooner treatments and services can begin.

**Response:**

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is diagnosed based on a combination of behavioral observations, developmental milestones, and medical history. There are no specific tests or screening tools that can diagnose ASD, but there are several standardized assessment tools that can help identify children who may be at risk for the condition. These include the Q-CHAT-10 and M-CHAT-R/F, which are designed to screen for autistic traits in young children.

It's important to note that a diagnosis of ASD requires a thorough clinical evaluation by a qualified professional, such as a developmental pediatrician or psychologist. This typically involves observing the child's behavior, interviewing parents and caregivers, and administering various assessments and tests. The goal of this evaluation is to determine whether the child meets the criteria for an ASD diagnosis and to develop an individualized treatment plan.
